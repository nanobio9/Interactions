Conical Boundary Problem — Eigenvalue Spectrum and Eigenfunctions

Jackson §3.4 · How the boundary condition \(P_\nu(\cos\beta) = 0\) determines the spectrum \(\{\nu_k\}\)

Spectrum \(\nu_k(\beta)\), \(k = 1,2,3,4\)
The \(k\)-th root of \(P_\nu(\cos\beta)=0\) as a function of the cone half-angle \(\beta\). Dotted horizontals: integer values (full-sphere limit \(\beta\to 180°\)). Dashed vertical: \(\beta=90°\) (flat conducting plane; \(\nu_1=1\) exactly).

As \(\beta\to 180°\) each \(\nu_k\) approaches an integer — the familiar Legendre spectrum is recovered. For a conical hole (\(\beta<90°\)), \(\nu_1>1\) and the field vanishes at the tip. For a conical point (\(\beta>90°\)), \(\nu_1<1\) and the field diverges — the mathematical basis for lightning rods.

In the scaled view (top) the zeros appear nearly stationary — they always divide \([0,\beta]\) in approximately the same relative positions. The absolute view (bottom) shows that the zeros physically move as \(\beta\) changes. Beyond \(\theta=\beta\) the functions continue as solutions of the differential equation, but with no boundary condition to organise them.

Further Discussion

Why are the zeros nearly evenly spaced inside \([0,\beta]\)? The functions \(P_{\nu_k}(\cos\theta)\) are eigenfunctions of a Sturm–Liouville problem on \([0,\beta]\), with boundary conditions \(P_{\nu_k}(1)=1\) at \(\theta=0\) and \(P_{\nu_k}(\cos\beta)=0\) at the conducting surface. The Sturm Oscillation Theorem guarantees that the \(k\)-th eigenfunction has exactly \(k-1\) zeros in the open interior \((0,\beta)\). This is not a special property of Legendre functions — it holds for every Sturm–Liouville problem.

The equal spacing, however, is only asymptotic: as \(k\to\infty\), the spacing between consecutive zeros approaches \(\beta/k\), but for small \(k\) the zeros are not exactly equally spaced. You can verify this directly in the figure above: drag \(\beta\) to \(90°\) and read off the zero locations of the \(k=3\) eigenfunction. They fall near \(25.3°\) and \(57.5°\), not at the equally-spaced values \(30°\) and \(60°\). The discrepancy is not a numerical error — it is a genuine feature of the low-order eigenfunctions.

The physical reason is instructive. In the short-wavelength limit (large \(k\)), the eigenfunction oscillates rapidly and behaves locally like a trigonometric function, whose zeros are exactly equally spaced. This is the same phenomenon as in a quantum mechanical infinite square well: the high-energy eigenstates \(\psi_n(x) \propto \sin(n\pi x/L)\) have perfectly equally spaced zeros, while the low-energy states in a more general potential do not. Equal spacing is a property of the semiclassical limit, not of exact eigenfunctions at finite \(k\).

The boundary condition at \(\beta\) is what confines this structure to the interval \([0,\beta]\). It forces each eigenfunction to turn around and return to zero within the prescribed domain. Without it, the zeros would not be confined or organised in any particular way.

Why does the pattern break beyond \(\theta=\beta\)? For \(\theta>\beta\), the function \(P_{\nu_k}(\cos\theta)\) continues as a solution of the Legendre equation, but it is no longer an eigenfunction of any boundary-value problem. No condition constrains it in \((\beta,\pi]\), so the oscillation theorem says nothing about its zeros there. The structure — the even spacing, the organised oscillation — was imposed from outside. Remove the boundary, and it disappears.

A parallel: spline interpolation in atomic physics. A classical numerical technique fits the atomic potential \(V(r)\) by a spline curve on a finite interval \([r_{\min},r_{\max}]\). Inside that interval the spline satisfies continuity and smoothness conditions at each knot, and the Schrödinger equation \[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V(r)\,\psi = E\psi \] can be solved reliably. But a spline extended beyond its defining interval has no constraints — it oscillates wildly or diverges. Wave functions computed from it beyond \(r_{\max}\) are meaningless. The parallel with \(P_{\nu_k}(\cos\theta)\) beyond \(\theta=\beta\) is exact: in both cases a finite interval with boundary conditions creates ordered, structured solutions; outside that interval, the order evaporates.

There is, however, an important difference. Sturm–Liouville eigenfunctions possess global structure that splines do not: orthogonality, \[ \int_0^\beta P_{\nu_k}(\cos\theta)\,P_{\nu_j}(\cos\theta)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0 \qquad (k\neq j), \] and the uniform zero-spacing guaranteed by the oscillation theorem. These follow from the self-adjoint structure of the differential operator — not merely from matching conditions at two endpoints. A spline is constructed to satisfy local conditions at knots; its global behaviour is incidental. An eigenfunction is determined globally by the operator and the boundary — the local behaviour is a consequence, not a design choice.
