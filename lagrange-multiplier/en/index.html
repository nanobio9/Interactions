From eliminating variables to parallel gradients

The constraint curve here is an ellipse rotated by \(\theta\), and the objective function is a family of hyperbolas rotated by \(\varphi\):

\[ g(x,y)=\frac{u^2}{a^2}+\frac{v^2}{b^2}-1=0,\qquad F(x,y)=p^2-q^2, \] \[ \begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}p\\q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi&\sin\varphi\\-\sin\varphi&\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}, \]

with \(a=2,\ b=1\). Every point, curve and number on this page is computed live from these parameters.

1. Eliminating a variable means writing the curve locally as y = y(x)

"Solve the constraint for \(y\) and substitute" amounts to picking a piece of the curve and representing it as a function \(y=y(x)\). Substituting gives a single-variable function \(f(x)=F(x,y(x))\), whose extremum condition is

\[ f'(x)=F_x+F_y\,\frac{dy}{dx}=0,\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{g_x}{g_y}. \]

The implicit function theorem guarantees that such a local representation exists wherever \(g_y\neq 0\). Where \(g_y=0\), it does not.

2. The tangent direction decides which representation works

\(g_y=0\) means \(\nabla g\) is horizontal, so the tangent is vertical. At such a point the curve turns back in \(x\): the two branches \(y_\pm(x)\) meet there and \(dy/dx\to\infty\). A closed curve like the ellipse always has two such points, and also two points with a horizontal tangent, where \(x=x(y)\) breaks down. The red circles in the plot mark the breakdown points for the chosen independent variable; in the one-dimensional plot on the right, the two branches meet at the red points with infinite slope.

3. You don't know which one to pick until you've found the extrema

If an extremum lies exactly where \(g_y=0\), it is an endpoint of the domain of \(f(x)\), not a root of \(f'(x)=0\), so solving \(f'(x)=0\) misses it. Press "Axis-aligned": now \(F=x^2-y^2\) and the constraint is \(x^2/4+y^2=1\).

  • With \(x\) independent: \(f(x)=\tfrac54x^2-1\), and \(f'(x)=0\) gives only the minimum at \(x=0\). The maximum sits at the endpoints \(x=\pm2\) and is missed.
  • With \(y\) independent: \(f(y)=4-5y^2\), and \(f'(y)=0\) gives only the maximum at \(y=0\). The minimum sits at the endpoints \(y=\pm1\) and is also missed.

Whichever variable you pick, one pair of extrema is lost. Now press "Both rotated 35°": the geometry is exactly the same, just rotated as a whole, and neither representation hits a breakdown point. So the breakdown is not a property of the problem itself; it comes from the choice of coordinates.

4. Lagrange: write down the geometric condition directly

At an extremum, \(F\) does not change as you move along the curve: \(\nabla F\cdot\hat T=0\). Since \(\nabla g\) is also perpendicular to the tangent \(\hat T\), the two gradients must be parallel:

\[ \nabla F=\lambda\,\nabla g,\qquad g(x,y)=0 . \]

These three equations treat \(x\) and \(y\) on an equal footing, so no independent variable has to be chosen in advance. The only requirement is \(\nabla g\neq 0\), i.e. the curve itself is smooth. As you drag \(P\), watch the angle between \(\nabla F\) and \(\nabla g\): it is 0° or 180° only at the extrema, no matter which representation breaks down there.

In this example both \(F=\mathbf r^{\mathsf T}M\mathbf r\) and \(g=\mathbf r^{\mathsf T}G\mathbf r-1\) are quadratic forms, so the Lagrange condition becomes the generalized eigenvalue problem \(M\mathbf r=\lambda G\mathbf r\). Multiplying both sides by \(\mathbf r^{\mathsf T}\) and using \(\mathbf r^{\mathsf T}G\mathbf r=1\) gives \(F=\lambda\): the multiplier itself is the extreme value.

Connection to mechanics. Think of \(g=0\) as a constraint on a particle (say, a bead sliding on a wire) and of \(-\nabla F\) as the applied force. In equilibrium, the component of the applied force along the wire must vanish, and the part perpendicular to the wire is balanced by the constraint force \(\lambda\nabla g\). The Lagrange multiplier is the magnitude of the constraint force (up to a factor of \(|\nabla g|\)), and it appears in exactly the same way in Lagrange's equations of motion.
